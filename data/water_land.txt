Water and land.
Water cycle: water evaporates from oceans, lakes, and land. It condenses into clouds. It falls as rain or snow. It flows in rivers, soaks into groundwater, or returns to oceans.
Freshwater is a small share of world water. Most water is salty ocean water. Freshwater in glaciers, groundwater, lakes, and rivers must be shared between people, farms, industry, and nature.
Water withdrawals are water taken from a source. Water consumption is water used and not returned. Withdrawals and consumption are different.
Agriculture accounts for about 70 percent of global freshwater withdrawals, according to FAO AQUASTAT and UNESCO. A 2025 study warns this familiar figure has wide uncertainty, with a possible range of 45 to 90 percent, and a central range of 70 to 85 percent. Agriculture is the largest reported user, but the exact share is less precise than headlines suggest.
Industry accounts for about 15 percent of withdrawals, and municipal use about 13 percent, in UN World Water Development Report 2025 sector data for 2021.
Producing food for one person per day requires around 2000 to 5000 litres of water, according to FAO. That includes water for crops, animals, and processing, not only drinking water.
A person needs 50 to 100 litres of clean freshwater per day for basic needs and health, according to WHO.
About 2.2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water in 2022, according to UN data. About 3.5 billion lacked safely managed sanitation.
About 4 billion people face severe water scarcity at least one month per year. Water scarcity means demand exceeds supply, or infrastructure and institutions fail to balance needs.
Women and girls spend an estimated 250 million hours every day collecting water, according to UN reports. That time is lost from school, work, and rest.
Groundwater is water below ground in aquifers. Pumping groundwater faster than it refills lowers water tables and dries wells.
Surface water is water in rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. Pollution from farms, industry, and sewage makes surface water unsafe.
Wastewater can be treated and reused for agriculture, if it is safe. Reuse reduces freshwater demand in dry regions.
Land.
Land use is how people use land. Land cover is what is physically on land, such as forest, cropland, grassland, or buildings.
In 2023, world agricultural land was 4800 million hectares, more than one-third of global land area, according to FAO.
Within agricultural land, cropland covered about 1600 million hectares, and permanent meadows and pastures covered 3200 million hectares.
Forest land covered 4050 million hectares in the same FAO land data, and other land covered 4150 million hectares.
Cropland per person is falling as population grows. More people share the same cropland, so yield per hectare matters more.
Forest area in 2025 was 4.14 billion hectares, about 32 percent of global land area, according to FAO Global Forest Resources Assessment 2025.
Deforestation was 10.9 million hectares per year in 2015 to 2025, down from 17.6 million hectares per year in 1990 to 2000.
Net forest loss was 4.12 million hectares per year in 2015 to 2025, down from 10.7 million hectares per year in 1990 to 2000. Net loss subtracts new forest from gross loss.
Forests store carbon, hold water, protect soil, and host biodiversity. Losing forest releases carbon and harms water and soil.
Land, water, soil, and climate together.
Land use changes water. Clearing forest changes rainfall, runoff, and soil moisture.
Irrigation changes water. Efficient irrigation, soil cover, and crop choice reduce water use per tonne of food.
Plan land and water together. A food plan needs land area, water availability, soil health, and weather, not land alone.
Protect forests, wetlands, and watersheds. They are water infrastructure, not only nature to protect after development.
