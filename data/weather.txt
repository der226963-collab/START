Weather.
Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place and time.
Climate is the pattern of weather over a long time, usually 30 years.
Weather is what you get. Climate is what you expect.
Weather variables.
Temperature measures how hot or cold air is, in degrees Celsius.
Precipitation is water falling from clouds. Rain, snow, and hail are precipitation.
Wind is moving air. Wind speed is measured in kilometres per hour or metres per second.
Wind direction tells where wind comes from. A north wind comes from the north.
Humidity is water vapour in air. Relative humidity is a percent of the maximum at that temperature.
Atmospheric pressure is the weight of air above a place, in hectopascals.
Cloud cover is how much sky is covered by cloud. Clear, partly cloudy, and overcast are cloud cover terms.
Visibility is how far you can see. Fog reduces visibility.
Instruments.
A thermometer measures temperature. A barometer measures pressure.
An anemometer measures wind speed. A rain gauge measures rainfall.
A hygrometer measures humidity. Satellites, radar, and weather stations collect data.
How weather forms.
The sun heats the Earth unevenly. Warm air rises. Cold air sinks and moves in to replace it.
That movement is wind. Warm, moist air rises and cools. Water vapour condenses into clouds.
Clouds grow and precipitation falls. High pressure often brings clear skies. Low pressure often brings cloud and rain.
Fronts are boundaries between air masses. A cold front can bring sudden rain and wind.
Seasons.
Seasons follow the tilt of the Earth and its orbit around the sun.
Summer is warmer with longer days. Winter is colder with shorter days.
Spring and autumn are transitions. Seasons are opposite in the northern and southern hemispheres.
Forecasting.
A forecast predicts future weather. Forecasts use observations and numerical models.
A numerical weather model divides the atmosphere into grid cells and calculates physics in each cell.
Short forecasts are more accurate than long forecasts. A 7 day forecast is less certain than a 1 day forecast.
Forecasts give probabilities. A 70 percent chance of rain means rain is likely, not certain.
Report a range for temperature. A range shows uncertainty.
Warnings protect people. Heat warnings, flood warnings, and storm warnings save lives when people act on them.
Weather and planning.
Weather affects food. Rain, frost, heat, and drought change crop growth and harvest.
Weather affects energy. Wind and sun change renewable generation. Heat and cold change demand for heating and cooling.
Weather affects buildings. Heat, flood, and wind damage buildings and harm health.
Weather affects health. Heatwaves harm older people, children, and outdoor workers first.
Weather affects transport. Snow, ice, fog, and storms delay travel and delivery.
Plan for weather, not only for averages. Plan for heat, flood, drought, and storm.
Extreme weather.
A heatwave is a long period of hot weather. Heatwaves become hotter and longer as climate warms.
Heavy rain becomes more likely as air warms. For every 1 degree Celsius of warming, air can hold about 7 percent more moisture.
More moisture can mean heavier rainfall when storms form. Flood risk rises in many places.
Drought is a long period with little rain. Drought reduces water, crops, and hydropower.
Wildfire risk rises when heat, drought, and wind combine. Fire spreads faster in dry, windy conditions.
A single cold week does not disprove warming. A single hot day does not prove a trend. Look at long records.
Weather, climate, and decisions.
Use weather data to plan a day or a week. Use climate data to plan a season or a year.
Use both to plan infrastructure. A building must survive storms, not only average weather.
Early warning systems need data, communication, and a plan to act. A warning without a plan does not protect people.
People decide how to prepare. Models and forecasts provide evidence. Assemblies decide priorities and resources.
