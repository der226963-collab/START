Soil science.
Soil is not dirt. Soil is a living system. It grows 95 percent of world food.
Soil has minerals, organic matter, water, and air. A typical soil is about 45 percent minerals, 5 percent organic matter, 25 percent water, and 25 percent air. These are general values, not exact for every soil.
Soil texture is sand, silt, and clay. Sand is coarse. Silt is medium. Clay is fine.
Loam is a mix of sand, silt, and clay. Loam holds water and nutrients well and drains well.
Soil structure is how particles group together. Good structure lets roots, water, and air move through soil.
Soil organic matter is dead plants, animals, and microbes, partly broken down.
Organic matter holds water, holds nutrients, feeds microbes, and stores carbon.
Healthy soil is dark, crumbles easily, and has earthworms and fungi. Degraded soil is pale, hard, and low in life.
Soil pH measures acidity. A pH of 7 is neutral. Below 7 is acid. Above 7 is alkaline.
Most crops grow well near pH 6 to 7. pH changes nutrient availability. Phosphorus is less available in very acid or very alkaline soil.
Soil nutrients.
Plants need macronutrients in large amounts. Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium are the main three, called NPK.
Nitrogen, N, builds leaves and proteins. Nitrogen deficiency makes leaves pale yellow and growth slow.
Phosphorus, P, builds roots, flowers, and energy transfer. Phosphorus deficiency makes growth slow and leaves dark or purple.
Potassium, K, helps water control, disease resistance, and fruit quality. Potassium deficiency makes leaf edges yellow or brown.
Calcium, magnesium, and sulfur are secondary nutrients. Plants need them in smaller amounts than NPK, but still need them.
Micronutrients are needed in tiny amounts. Iron, zinc, copper, boron, manganese, molybdenum, and nickel are micronutrients.
A lack of a micronutrient can limit growth even if NPK is high. Balance matters more than a single nutrient.
A common target ratio for NPK fertiliser use is 4 to 2 to 1. That is 4 parts nitrogen, 2 parts phosphorus, 1 part potassium.
When the ratio is distorted, for example too much nitrogen and too little potassium, crops and soil suffer.
Nutrient cycles.
Nitrogen cycle: nitrogen gas in air is fixed by bacteria and by industry into reactive nitrogen plants can use.
Legumes host bacteria that fix nitrogen in roots. Clover, beans, and peas are legumes.
Manure, compost, and crop residues return nutrients to soil. Recycling nutrients reduces waste.
Phosphorus cycle: phosphorus comes from rock. It moves through soil, plants, animals, and water.
Phosphorus is finite. Mined phosphorus does not regrow quickly. Waste phosphorus in water causes problems.
Potassium cycle: potassium comes from minerals in soil and rock. It is lost by harvest, erosion, and leaching.
Nutrient balance.
Nutrient balance equals inputs minus outputs minus losses. Inputs are fertiliser, manure, compost, and fixation.
Outputs are harvest and removal. Losses are leaching, runoff, erosion, and gas.
If inputs exceed outputs and losses, nutrients build up in soil and water. If inputs are less, soil is mined and fertility falls.
A surplus in one place and a deficit in another place is common. Some regions use too much fertiliser. Other regions use too little.
Excess nitrogen and phosphorus in water cause algal blooms. Algae die, oxygen falls, and fish die. This is eutrophication.
Nitrous oxide is a greenhouse gas from nitrogen in soil. Excess nitrogen increases emissions.
Soil and climate.
Soil stores carbon. Soil carbon sequestration moves carbon dioxide from air into soil organic matter.
Healthy soil with more organic matter holds more water. That helps crops in drought and reduces flood runoff.
Erosion removes topsoil. Wind and water carry soil away. Bare soil erodes faster than covered soil.
Compaction squeezes soil. Heavy machines and animals compact soil. Compacted soil holds less water and air.
Salinization adds salt to soil. Irrigation with salty water and poor drainage cause salinization.
Acidification lowers soil pH. Acid soil reduces nutrient availability and root growth.
Soil degradation.
About 33 percent of global soils are moderately to highly degraded, according to FAO.
Degradation causes include erosion, salinization, compaction, acidification, and chemical pollution.
Degraded soil can lose about 20 to 25 percent of staple crop yield, according to FAO guidance.
If current trends continue, FAO warns degraded soils could rise to 90 percent by 2050. That is a warning, not a forecast of fate.
Degradation is reversible in many places. Cover crops, rotation, compost, and reduced tillage can rebuild soil.
Planetary boundaries for nutrients.
Nitrogen and phosphorus are planetary boundaries, called biogeochemical flows.
Both boundaries have been substantially exceeded, according to the Planetary Health Check.
Phosphorus application to cropland is about 18.2 teragrams of phosphorus per year, about 3 times the regional boundary of 6.2 teragrams per year.
Nitrogen fixation is about 165 teragrams of nitrogen per year, more than twice its boundary, in the high-risk zone.
Exceeding these boundaries does not mean farming must stop. It means nutrient use must be balanced, targeted, and recycled.
Soil testing and planning.
Test soil before adding fertiliser. A soil test measures pH, organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients.
Apply only what the crop and soil need. Right nutrient, right amount, right place, right time.
Rotate crops. Different crops use different nutrients and break pest cycles.
Grow cover crops in off seasons. Cover crops protect soil, add organic matter, and fix nitrogen if they are legumes.
Add compost and manure. They add nutrients and organic matter together.
Keep soil covered. Mulch and living plants protect soil from erosion and heat.
Soil, weather, and food together.
Weather changes soil moisture. Rain, drought, and heat change nutrient availability and crop growth.
Healthy soil buffers weather. It holds water in drought and drains in heavy rain.
Plan food, soil, water, and climate together. A plan that ignores soil will fail, even if it has enough land and water on paper.
People decide soil use. Farmers, communities, and assemblies decide what to grow, how to fertilise, and how to protect soil.
Models can check a nutrient balance. They cannot replace soil testing, local knowledge, or a decision by people who live on the land.
